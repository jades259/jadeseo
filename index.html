Does hosting the Olympics pay off?

An independent research paper on whether the Olympics and the World Cup pay off for the countries that host them.

The paper looks at three things: projected versus actual budgets for past Olympic and World Cup hosts, what happens to the venues after the crowds leave, and the opportunity cost of the public money involved. I am the sole author; a biostatistician at Johns Hopkins Hospital reviewed the draft.

The question I ended up with is the one I want to keep asking in college: not whether the games bring money in, but what else that money could have built.

Read the paper (PDF)Download the data (Excel)

What the paper does

  • Builds a dataset of 91 events, 1988 to 2025
  • Compares projected and actual host budgets
  • Tracks venue use after the event
  • Compares hosts’ GDP growth with the countries that bid and lost
  • Weighs the opportunity cost of public spending
  • Sole author, 2025 to 2026; reviewed by a Johns Hopkins Hospital biostatistician

Benched: what happens to the college athletes who never go pro

A student documentary by Young Global Leaders of the East. It follows three former Division I athletes through the moment the dream of going pro ended.

Released October 30, 2026; re-uploaded November 4, 2026. The athletes: a former Division I women’s tennis player who asked not to be named, Julian Bell (Clemson women’s lacrosse), and Spencer Joyner (Boston University men’s basketball).

I am the film’s Research and Policy Lead. I researched athletic scholarship structures, academic clustering (the way athletes get steered into certain majors), athlete welfare policy, and which schools already run programs for athletes after sport. I am drafting the policy brief that comes with the film: what every school should owe the people who play for it.

I have played varsity golf for four years and captain the team this season. I am not going pro either, which is part of why this question stayed with me.

ShiftBoard, after the Commission meeting

A blind student learns how a graph moves by feeling it. On ShiftBoard, each graph is a raised curve piece that fits into a board with tactile axes and braille labels, so a student can feel the same curve move, flip, or mirror. After the meeting, I modeled and 3D printed the curve pieces, revised the board itself, tested the prototypes, and added the exponential and logarithmic pair.
